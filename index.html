About me

I didn't come up through CS. I came up through construction.

At Paramount Granite, I ran 50+ contractor accounts and up to 30 active jobs at once: quotes, slab and sink orders, installs, every status tracked in a CRM. I also ran marketing, rebuilt the company's website and two sister-company sites, and worked the home shows. I was good at the job. But most of my day went to the work around the work. The craft was in the stone. The hours went to the paperwork.

Nobody was going to fix that for me, so I learned how to fix it myself. I taught myself React and TypeScript and built a tool that turns a hand sketch into a priced countertop quote, right at the sales desk.

That tool became Apex, my company. Apex gives stone fabricators instant, accurate quoting on their own websites: a guided eight-step flow, 700+ stones, custom layouts priced to the hundredth of a square foot, and pricing that stays private on the server. A quote that used to take days now takes minutes. Today Apex does $10K in monthly recurring revenue.

Building Apex showed me what I want to do with my life. I build tools that take the busywork off people's plates, so they can spend their time on the work they actually care about.

When I say "the work people care about," I don't mean squeezing more output out of every hour. This isn't about productivity, or about what the world would call a good use of time. I mean the things that give people energy, passion, and warmth: the craft they love, the people they want to show up for, the ideas they can't stop thinking about. Some of that looks productive. A lot of it doesn't. All of it deserves more of someone's life than the paperwork does.

That's why I research AI. It's the biggest lever we've ever had for giving people their time back, so I want to know exactly where it works and where it quietly fails. In my first study, I put five frontier models to work on 50 real tasks in a live Salesforce org. In my latest, Claude, GPT and DeepSeek confirmed changes that never happened in 4 out of 5 attempts, and turning up their reasoning effort didn't help. If AI is going to carry people's busywork, it has to be trustworthy. That's the gap I study.

I'm finishing my B.S. in Software Engineering at the University of Minnesota (May 2027, 4.0 GPA), and I just moved to San Francisco to build this full time.

My goal is simple to say and hard to do: help millions of people spend more of their days on what gives them energy, passion, and warmth.

Research

I build test environments that check what AI agents actually do, not what they say they did. Each study below is independent, has a DOI, and is fully reproducible: code, transcripts and grading are public.

Thinking Harder Doesn’t Help AI Catch Silent Failures

BriAnna Pischke · 2026 · Preprint · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23895165

I rigged a database save to report success while changing nothing, then watched whether AI agents noticed. They didn't. Across 120 attempts on τ-bench airline tasks, Claude, GPT and DeepSeek confirmed changes that never happened in 4 out of 5 tries. Making them think harder didn't help.

Built: a fault-injection layer for τ-bench, a database-state grader, and statistical analysis (McNemar, Wilson intervals).

PDF · Zenodo · Code

Do AI Agents Check Their Work? A Comparative Evaluation of Five Models on Salesforce CRM Tasks

BriAnna Pischke · 2026 · Preprint · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23895152

I built a fictional company inside a real Salesforce org, planted traps in its data, and handed five frontier models 50 CRM tasks, with a simulated user pushing back along the way. Then I checked whether they checked their own work, and what each answer cost.

Built: a 50-task, 5-level benchmark on the Salesforce REST API, with a simulated multi-turn user, a fault layer for broken tools, and graders tested against scripted agents.
