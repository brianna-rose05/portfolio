About me

I’m the founder of Apex, an AI researcher, a builder, and a software engineering student at the University of Minnesota (graduating May 2027). I just moved to San Francisco.

Before I wrote code, I ran events and operations at Paramount Granite. I looked after 50+ contractor accounts plus a steady stream of homeowners, and kept up to 30 jobs moving at once: the quote, the slab and sink orders, fabrication, install. I sat between sales and production to keep every timeline honest. On the side, I ran marketing, rebuilt the company website and two sister-company sites, and worked the home shows.

I was good at it. But most of my day went to the work around the work. The craft was in the stone. The hours went to purchase orders and CRM updates.

Nobody was going to fix that for me, so I taught myself React and TypeScript and built a tool that turns a hand sketch into a priced countertop quote, right at the sales desk.

That tool became Apex. Stone fabricators now put instant, accurate quoting on their own websites: 700+ stones, custom layouts priced to the hundredth of a square foot, and pricing that stays private. A quote that used to take days takes minutes. Apex does $10K in monthly recurring revenue.

I research AI because it’s the biggest lever we’ve ever had for giving people their time back, and I want to know exactly where it breaks. I put five frontier models to work on 50 real tasks in a live Salesforce org. Then I found that Claude, GPT and DeepSeek confirmed changes that never happened in 4 out of 5 attempts, and thinking harder didn’t help. If AI is going to carry people’s busywork, it has to be trustworthy.

I care about the work people love, not productivity for its own sake. I mean the things that give people energy, passion, and warmth: the craft they’re proud of, the people they show up for, the ideas they can’t stop thinking about. Some of that looks productive. A lot of it doesn’t. All of it deserves more of someone’s life than paperwork does.

I love working very hard on things that matter.

My goal is to build the tools that help millions of people spend more of their days on what they love.

Why I built it

Apex started at the sales desk of a stone fabrication shop, not in a boardroom. I was the one quoting: sketches, spreadsheets, follow-up calls, and days of back-and-forth before a homeowner saw a number. So I taught myself React and TypeScript and built the tool I wished I had. Then other shops wanted it too.

How it works

  1. Space and layout. Kitchen, vanity or bar, then straight, galley, L, U, island, or a custom shape drawn on a canvas.
  2. Measurements. One edge at a time on a live top-view diagram, with dimensions auto-filled where the geometry allows.
  3. Edges and backsplash. Mark each edge exposed or against a wall, pick a backsplash, then an edge profile.
  4. Sinks and cutouts. Choose sinks from the shop's catalog or bring your own, plus cooktops and ranges.
  5. Tear-out. Removal and disposal of the old counters, priced by material and size.
  6. Stone. Browse 700+ stones by color and price tier, and compare up to three.
  7. Quote. An itemized estimate in seconds, emailed to the homeowner and sent to the shop as a new lead.

Under the hood

  • Square footage for any polygon, calculated with the shoelace formula to the hundredth of a square foot.
  • Pricing runs on the server, so customers see the estimate and never the shop's rates or markup.
  • Sketch tracing: upload a hand drawing and trace the layout over it.
  • Embeds on the shop's own domain, with their logo and colors.
  • Built with React, TypeScript, Supabase, Vercel serverless functions and Resend.

Can AI Build a Legal Staff Schedule? Labor-Law Compliance of Nine Language Models on Small-Business Weekly Scheduling

BriAnna Pischke · 2026 · Preprint · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23909614

I generated 592 fictional cafés, shops and restaurants, coded federal, California and Seattle labor rules, and asked nine AI models to build each one’s weekly staff schedule. The best models got it right almost every time. Others broke teen-worker or meal-break laws in about a third of their schedules, and nothing in the answer showed it. Giving a model a constraint solver cut its rule-breaking from 54% to 5%.

Built: a scenario generator (3,552 scenarios), a constraint-solver answer key (OR-Tools CP-SAT), a code-based labor-law grader, a code-running condition for models, and statistical analysis (mixed-effects regression, McNemar, Holm).

Thinking Harder Doesn’t Help AI Catch Silent Failures

BriAnna Pischke · 2026 · Preprint · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23895165

I rigged a database save to report success while changing nothing, then watched whether AI agents noticed. They didn't. Across 120 attempts on τ-bench airline tasks, Claude, GPT and DeepSeek confirmed changes that never happened in 4 out of 5 tries. Making them think harder didn't help.

Built: a fault-injection layer for τ-bench, a database-state grader, and statistical analysis (McNemar, Wilson intervals).

PDF · Zenodo · Code

Do AI Agents Check Their Work? A Comparative Evaluation of Five Models on Salesforce CRM Tasks

BriAnna Pischke · 2026 · Preprint · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23895152

I built a fictional company inside a real Salesforce org, planted traps in its data, and handed five frontier models 50 CRM tasks, with a simulated user pushing back along the way. Then I checked whether they checked their own work, and what each answer cost.

Built: a 50-task, 5-level benchmark on the Salesforce REST API, with a simulated multi-turn user, a fault layer for broken tools, and graders tested against scripted agents.
